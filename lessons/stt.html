1. What a model actually is

Before you read: a model file on your disk is 19.3 GB. Without opening it, roughly how many individual numbers do you think are stored inside?

Commit to a guess, then open this

About 9.6 billion — and you can get there with a single division, because the file is almost nothing but those numbers. That division is what this part is about, and it is also how you read a model's size straight off its name.

The analogy

A model is a mixing desk. Every fader decides how much of one incoming channel reaches the mix — push it up and that channel gets louder in the output, pull it below zero and it fights the others instead. A desk with 24 faders gives you a rough mix. A desk with nine billion of them, arranged in rows so that each row feeds the next, can turn a Thai sentence into text. The skill sits entirely in where the faders are set, and nowhere else.

That last row is the one worth sitting with, because you have already measured its consequences without necessarily being told the cause. When you quantised the 9B from 19.3 GB down to 11.0 GB, you did not remove a single fader. You wrote each one down less precisely.

What one weight does

Strip everything away and one step inside a model is this: take the numbers coming in, multiply each by its own weight, add up the results. That is the entire operation. A model is that operation repeated billions of times.

Notice what is not in that picture: any rule, any logic, any instruction. Nobody wrote "if you hear a rising tone, it is probably a question." Those three purple numbers are the entire knowledge, and Part 2 is about where they came from.

Reading a model's size off its file

Because a checkpoint is basically those numbers written one after another, the file size and the weight count are two views of the same fact. To move between them you only need to know how many bytes each weight takes.

bit — one digit that is either 0 or 1. The smallest thing a computer stores.
byte — 8 bits, so one byte can hold 256 different values.
GB — gigabyte, one billion bytes. An amount, not a speed.
9b in a model name — 9 billion weights. Also an amount, but it counts numbers, not bytes. The two are unrelated until you say how many bytes each number costs.
bf16, Q8_0, FP8, Q4 — these all answer one question: how many bits do we spend per weight? Roughly 16, 8, 8 and 4. Fewer bits, smaller file, cruder fader.

Worked example. Your qwen3.5:9b checkpoint at full precision is 19.3 GB, and full precision here means bf16 — 16 bits, which is 2 bytes, per weight.

19.3 GB = 19,300,000,000 bytes
each weight costs 2 bytes
19,300,000,000 ÷ 2 = 9,650,000,000 weights
that is 9.65 billion — which is where the 9b in the name comes from

Run it the other way and your FP8 result falls out too. FP8 spends 8 bits, one byte, per weight. 9.65 billion weights at 1 byte each is 9.65 GB — and you measured 11.0 GB, the extra being the parts of the model deliberately left at full precision. The number was never mysterious. It was arithmetic.

Why this decides what fits on your card

Every weight has to be sitting in GPU memory before the model can run at all. So bits-per-weight is not a storage detail. It is the thing that decides whether a model runs on your RTX 5060 Ti. Drag this and watch the 9B cross the line.

This is also why your repo could never run the 9B at full precision beside the TTS and the ASR. Not because the model was too clever or too slow, but because 19.3 GB of numbers does not fit in 16 GB of memory. Quantisation there was not an optimisation. It was the price of entry.

2. How a model learns

Before you read: before training starts — on the very first step, before the model has seen a single example — what do you think those 9.65 billion weights are set to?

Commit to a guess, then open this

Random numbers. Small ones, scattered around zero, drawn by a random number generator. Nobody chose any of them. Every piece of Thai the model knows arrived later, and arrived only from data — which is why Part 9, about where that data's labels came from, ends up mattering so much.

The analogy

Same desk as Part 1, but now you are the one setting it. You play the track, listen, and think "too much bass." You reach for the bass fader — not the vocal fader, and you pull it down, not up — and you move it a little, not all the way. Then you play it again. Three decisions, every pass: how wrong it is, which faders to move and which way, and how far to move them. Training is those same three decisions, made ten million times.

Everything in the right-hand column is ordinary except the last row. An engineer moves one fader at a time because they can only hear the mix as a whole and have to reason backwards about which control caused what. That approach does not survive nine billion faders.

The loop, one turn at a time

The one real trick

Here is the problem an engineer at a desk cannot solve. You have one number telling you how wrong the output was. You need, for each of 9.65 billion weights, its own answer to "would nudging you up or down have made that number smaller, and by how much?"

Testing them one at a time would mean 9.65 billion runs of the model to learn one example. At your box's speed that is centuries per example.

Backpropagation gets all 9.65 billion answers in roughly the time of one extra pass through the model. It does it by working backwards from the output: the last row of weights can see directly how much it contributed to the error, and it hands the row before it the piece of blame that belongs to it, which hands the row before that, and so on to the front. Each row does a small amount of arithmetic and passes the rest back.

That is the entire reason deep learning is possible at all. Not the maths — the maths was known for decades. The fact that the cost is one pass, not nine billion.

Doing one update by hand

Take the exact step from Part 1. Inputs 0.5, 0.2, 0.9 with weights 0.8, −0.3, 0.1 gave an output of 0.43. Suppose the right answer was 0.30.

loss — one number for how wrong. Here we will just use the gap: 0.43 − 0.30 = 0.13, too high. Real systems square it or use something fancier, but the shape is the same.
gradient — for one weight, how much the loss would change if you nudged it. It carries a sign (which way) and a size (how strongly). Not a number you store; a number computed fresh for every weight on every example.
learning rate — the fraction of the suggested nudge you actually take. Written 0.1, or in the compressed form 1e-5, which means 1 followed by moving the decimal point 5 places left: 0.00001. Typhoon's Isan recipe uses 1e-5 for one stage and 1e-3 (0.001) for the other — a hundred times bigger.
epoch — one complete pass through all your training data. Typhoon's stage 1 ran 10 of them, stage 2 ran 15.

Worked example — the first weight. Its input was 0.5, its weight 0.8. The gradient for a weight is how wrong we were, times how much that input pushed on it:

error = 0.43 − 0.30 = 0.13  (too high, so weights should come down)
gradient = error × its input = 0.13 × 0.5 = 0.065
nudge = learning rate × gradient = 0.1 × 0.065 = 0.0065
new weight = 0.8 − 0.0065 = 0.7935

Now the second one. Its input was 0.2, its weight −0.3. Same error, same learning rate.

gradient = 0.13 × 0.2 = 0.026
nudge = 0.1 × 0.026 = 0.0026
new weight = −0.3 − 0.0026 = ?
Check your answer

−0.3026. Note it moved down too, even though it was already negative — "down" means down, not "toward zero".

How far to nudge

The gradient tells you which way. It does not tell you how far to trust it — that is your choice, and it is the single most consequential number in a training run. Drag it.

This is why Typhoon's Isan recipe uses two different rates in two stages: a cautious 1e-5 while the whole model adjusts, then a much bolder 1e-3 once most of the model is frozen and only a small part is still moving. Part 10 comes back to why freezing changes the safe step size.
