1. What the model actually hears

Before you read. Your microphone hands you 16,000 numbers every second, and each one only says how far the speaker cone moved at that instant. The encoder takes 80 ms of that and hands back a row of 512 numbers.

Whatever you guessed — the thing to hold onto is that they are no longer sound. You cannot play them back. They are a description. The rest of this part is about what they describe.

The analogy

You already built this exact thing. Every Thai turn your panel handles gets written to live/_out/turns-<date>.jsonl. The turn itself was maybe two seconds of 48 kHz audio — megabytes. What you keep is six marks and five stage names: a short row of numbers you can read at a glance. You threw the sound away and kept a description of it.

The encoder does the same move, just much smaller and much more often.

You wrote the logger, so you already know this shape

That last row is the whole point, so it is worth saying plainly. Once the audio has gone through the encoder, the sound is gone. Nothing downstream ever touches it again. The decoder, the part that picks Thai characters, has never heard audio in its life — it only ever sees rows of numbers.

16 kHz — 16,000 samples per second. A sample is one number saying where the speaker cone was at one instant. k = kilo = one thousand. The per second makes it a rate, not an amount.
80 ms — 80 milliseconds, that is 0.08 of a second. An amount of time.
So one slice holds 16,000 × 0.08 = 1,280 samples. That is an amount, not a rate.

Your own loop already works in slices like this — asr/server_streaming.py feeds the model in chunks rather than whole files. So the slicing is familiar. What is new is what comes out of a slice.

Notice what happened as you dragged. Over silence, most of the 512 sit near zero and a few stay lit — the encoder is still saying something, roughly "nothing is being said here, and the room sounds like this." Over speech, many more light up, in a pattern that changes as the sound changes.

Nobody sat down and decided that number 137 means "this slice has a nasal sound in it". Training decided, over 11,000 hours of Thai, and no human ever looked. The numbers are not labelled and cannot be read by eye. But they are consistent: the same kind of sound produces a similar row every time, which is exactly what makes the rest of the model possible.

One sentence to carry into part 2: the encoder is a machine that turns a slice of sound into a row of 512 numbers, and everything after it works with the row, never the sound.

2. What “frozen” means

Before you read. From part 3 you know that switching the adapter off gives you back the original numbers exactly.

Guess first: if the encoder were not frozen while you trained on synthetic audio, would switching the adapter off still give you the original encoder back?

Commit to a guess, then open this

No. And there is no second switch to fix it. That one sentence is the entire reason freezing exists, and the rest of this part is why.

The analogy

Back to the mixing desk from your first lesson. A model is a desk with millions of faders, and one fader is one number. Training is somebody sitting at the desk: play a clip, listen to how wrong it came out, nudge some faders, play the next clip. Do that a few hundred thousand times and the desk is set.

Freezing is putting tape over a block of faders. The engineer still reaches for them, still tries to push — and nothing moves. So every correction they wanted to make has to be made on a fader that is still free.

Same desk as your first lesson, one thing added
At the mixing deskIn the model
one fader one weight — a single number inside the model
play, listen, nudge, repeat training — show an example, measure how wrong, nudge the numbers
tape over a block of faders frozen — those numbers are not allowed to change
the engineer pushes anyway, nothing moves training still computes a nudge for them; it gets thrown away
the correction lands on whatever fader is still free every lesson in the data lands on a number that can move
you can peel the tape off later frozen is a choice you make per run, not a property of the model

That fifth row is the one that matters, so here it is on its own:

A lesson has to land somewhere. Freezing decides where.

M = million. 109 M is 109 million separate numbers — an amount, not a rate. For scale: Typhoon's encoder holds about 109 M, the decoder and joint network together about 6 M, and the adapter we would bolt on about 1 M. In the picture below, one square is roughly one million numbers, drawn to scale.

Look at the sizes for a second, because they are honest. The adapter is one square against the encoder's hundred and nine. It is not a small model bolted onto a big one — it is barely a rounding error. That is only possible because it is not trying to do the encoder's job. It is only trying to catch what falls out of the synthetic clips.

Why this is the answer to your question

You asked whether the adapter is just a cheap way to fine-tune the encoder without wrecking it. For most people using adapters, yes. For us, the encoder is not being fine-tuned at all. It is being held completely still.

And that is deliberate, because of what part 1 and the two-failure split already told you. A synthetic clip carries two lessons: how วโรรส is spelled (worth keeping) and what VoxCPM's fake silences sound like (poison). If every group of numbers is free to move, both lessons go wherever they like, mixed together, and you can never separate them again.

Tape the encoder and the poison has nowhere else to go. The only free numbers on the sound side belong to the adapter. So it collects there — and the adapter is the one thing in the whole model you can delete.

Freeze and adapter are one mechanism, not two. The tape is what makes the bin a bin. Without it, there is no bin, just a bigger mess.

Check yourself: what does freezing the encoder buy you here?

True, and genuinely useful — it is why this fits on your 5060 Ti at all. But it is a side effect, not the reason. If speed were the goal you would just train on less data. Ask instead: what becomes possible that was not possible before?

Right. Freezing does not stop bad learning — it decides where bad learning is allowed to accumulate. Somewhere with an off-switch.

It cannot stop that. The model will happily learn VoxCPM's fake silences either way — nothing here inspects a lesson and judges it. What changes is only which numbers are available to hold it.

3. What the adapter adds

Before you read. Your mint_v4 voice is a LoRA — which is an adapter. When you set VOXCPM_LORA="" you get the plain VoxCPM voice back.

Guess first: does the model go back to exactly what it was before, or only approximately?

Commit to a guess, then open this

Exactly. Byte for byte, the same model you downloaded. Why that is true — and why it is the single most useful property an adapter has — is this whole part.

The analogy

You have been running one of these in production for months. mint_v4 is not a retrained VoxCPM — the VoxCPM weights on your disk were never touched. It is a small separate file that sits beside the model and shifts what comes out. Set VOXCPM_LORA="" and the shifting stops; the original voice returns, unharmed, because it was never edited in the first place.

The adapter we want to put in the ASR is the same arrangement, aimed at a different problem.

The mechanism behind all five of those rows is one word, and it is worth its own line because everything later depends on it:

The adapter adds. It never replaces.

Here is what that means concretely. From part 1 you know the encoder hands back a row of 512 numbers describing one slice of sound. The adapter looks at that row and produces its own row of 512 numbers — small ones. Then the two rows are added together, position by position, and the sum is what carries on to the decoder.

So the original row is still in there. It was nudged, not overwritten. And if the adapter's row happens to be all zeros, the sum is the original — not close to it, identical to it.

Drag the strength dial and watch the bottom row lean further away from the top one. That dial is not invented for this page — it is a setting in your own VoxCPM config.

r — how narrow the detour is. mint_v4 uses 32.
alpha — how far up the correction is turned before it gets added.
The correction is multiplied by alpha ÷ r before the addition. At 32/32 that is ×1.0 — full strength. At 16/32 it would be ×0.5, and the voice would come out only half-shifted toward mint_v4. That is exactly the “loads at half strength” warning sitting in your CLAUDE.md — and it can only happen to something that adds. A replacement has no such thing as half.

One honest note on the map above: a LoRA and the kind of adapter we would put in the ASR are not built identically inside. But on every point that matters here — base untouched, small separate weights, additive, switchable, scaled by alpha ÷ r — they behave the same way, which is why your months with mint_v4 transfer.

4. The bend — why

Before you read. The adapter's job is to catch VoxCPM's quirks. But there is more than one quirk: its silences are too clean, and its timing is too regular — and those show up in completely different slices of audio.

Guess first: can one correction, applied the same way to every slice, catch both?

Commit to a guess, then open this

No. And what it does instead is worse than doing nothing, because what it fails to catch does not stay put — it goes on to the decoder, which is the half you wanted to keep.

The analogy

You already wrote this exact distinction. cap_internal_silence() in stt_typhoon.py shortens a silence only if it is longer than 800 ms — it looks first, then decides. The dumb version would be “subtract 800 ms from every silence”, and you know exactly what that would do: it would wreck every normal pause in the clip while still not handling the pathological ones properly.

Your rule checks before it acts. The bend is what lets the adapter check before it acts.

Your own silence fix, and the same shape one level down
In cap_internal_silence()In the adapter
it measures the gap before touching it each detector checks whether its pattern is present
acts only when the gap is too long contributes only when it recognises something
short pauses pass through untouched slices that match nothing get no correction
“subtract 800 ms from everything”the version you did not write squeeze and widen with no bend in between
that would damage normal speech that lets most of the TTS-ness slip past the bin

Forget 512. Start with one number.

Everything below uses a single number going in and a single number coming out. Drag the slider and watch what the adapter is able to do — and what it is not.

Bend off, drag the slider and watch the output. It follows the input, always. Halve the input, the output halves. Flip the input to negative, the output flips too. There is no setting anywhere on that slider where the output is zero except dead centre. The output is chained to the input.

Now turn the bend on and drag again. Across the entire left half of the slider, the output is flat zero. Not small. Zero. The adapter is saying nothing here, I have no opinion, leave this one alone. Then past the corner it starts responding.

A straight line can stretch or flip. It can never say “nothing”.

That is the entire difference, and everything else in this part is that one fact repeated 32 times.

There is a second consequence worth knowing, because it explains why every model is built this way. Stack two straight lines and you get… a straight line. Stack ten and you still have a straight line. Without a corner somewhere, adding more steps buys you literally nothing — Typhoon's 17 layers would collapse into the work of one. The corners are the only reason depth means anything.

Now scale it from one to 32

The real adapter has 32 of those middle numbers, not one. Each behaves exactly like the slider you just dragged — it gets a number, and with the bend on it either stays at zero or speaks up. Call each one a detector: it is looking for one particular thing, and its number says how much of that thing is in this slice.

Now the whole question of this part is:

is a detector allowed to stay silent?

Without the bend, no. All 32 always have a say. Every correction the adapter makes is a blend of all thirty-two patterns at once, every single time. It cannot produce a correction aimed at clean silence without dragging the other thirty-one along with it.

With the bend, yes. The bend is the simplest possible rule: any detector whose number came out below zero is set to exactly zero. Not small — zero. It contributes nothing at all. So the ones that did not recognise anything stand down, and what is left is a correction aimed at what is actually in this slice.

Why this decides whether the bin works

Here is the part that connects to what you said a moment ago — the decoder learns the words, the encoder learns the fake voice.

The adapter is the bin standing in for the taped encoder. Its job is to catch the fake-voice lesson before it goes anywhere else. But a bin that can only make one blended, unfocused correction catches only part of what it is aimed at. Whatever it misses does not vanish. It carries on up to the decoder — and the decoder is free to move, so it learns it.

Which means you end up with fake-voice knowledge sitting in the one place with no off-switch, mixed in with the vocabulary you actually wanted. That is the naive mix again, arrived at by a more expensive route.

No bend → the bin leaks → the poison ends up where you cannot delete it.

Check yourself: what actually goes wrong without the bend?

5. The zero start — why

Before you read. You have bolted the adapter on but trained it for zero steps. Nothing has been taught yet.

Guess first: at that exact moment, if the adapter's numbers started out random, is the model better than plain Typhoon, the same, or worse?

Commit to a guess, then open this

Worse. You have damaged a model that cost 11,000 hours, before teaching it a single thing. This part is about what that damage actually costs you — and it is not what you would guess.

The analogy

You branch off main. The branch starts identical — not similar, identical — and every commit after that is a change you made on purpose and can read back with git diff main. That is the whole value of branching from the right place.

Now imagine branching from a random commit in somebody else's repository instead. You can still commit. Your work might even be good. But nothing you do is a clean diff against main any more, and merging it back drags in a thousand changes you never made.

The lane the synthetic audio takes is a branch, and you choose where it starts
In gitIn the adapter
main the real-audio lane — plain Typhoon, untouched
your branch the synthetic-audio lane — Typhoon plus the adapter
branching from mainstarts byte-identical starting the adapter at zeroboth lanes produce the same numbers
each commit, a deliberate change each training step, a deliberate correction
branching from a random commit starting the adapter at random
nothing is a clean diff any more the two lanes were never related to begin with

From part 3: the adapter starts by adding zero, so at the very first step the synthetic lane and the real lane produce exactly the same numbers. Same input, same output, no difference anywhere.

That is the point. The synthetic lane is a branch of the real one. It only moves away from it as far as the adapter learns it needs to, and every bit of that movement is something training put there deliberately.

What the random start actually costs you

Not training time. Something worse.

Remember what the decoder is for — in your own words, it is the part that learns the new words. It learns them by being shown a row of numbers and told which characters go with it. And it is shared: the same decoder sits at the end of both lanes.

With a zero start, a synthetic clip arrives at the decoder looking almost exactly like a real clip would. So when the decoder learns “numbers like these mean วโรรส”, that lesson still holds when a real caller says it. The lesson transfers, because the two lanes are relatives.

With a random start, a synthetic clip arrives looking like nothing a real caller ever produces. The decoder still learns something — but what it learns is “garbage of this particular flavour means วโรรส”. A real caller never produces that flavour of garbage. The lesson does not transfer. You paid for the training and got nothing.

Zero start makes the synthetic lane a branch of the real one. Random start makes it a different repo.

And there is a second reason, which matters for the way you work. If arm 3 of the experiment comes out worse than the untrained baseline, you need to be able to say why. With a zero start there is a guaranteed floor: the worst case is that training pushes the adapter back toward zero and you land roughly where you began — an honest null result. With a random start there is no floor at all, and a bad number tells you nothing, because you can no longer separate “the idea failed” from “I started somewhere broken”. Your own smartturn table is exactly this problem: every fine-tune landed below the untrained model, and the table could not say which of the two had happened.

Check yourself: what does a random start actually cost?

A little, and it is the answer most people give — but time is the cheap part. Ask instead what happens to the lesson you were paying for.

Right. The two lanes stop being relatives, so anything learned on one side is not information about the other. The training runs, the loss goes down, and none of it reaches a real caller.

It can learn perfectly sensible things, given enough steps. The damage is not to the adapter — it is to what the shared decoder takes away from the synthetic half.

6. The two lanes, end to end

Before you read. Training is finished and you delete the adapter. Three groups of numbers went into this: the encoder, the adapter, the decoder.

Guess first: which of them are different from when you started?

Commit to a guess, then open this

Exactly one. The encoder is byte-identical to what you downloaded, the adapter is in the bin, and the decoder is the only thing that changed. If that surprises you, this part is short.

The analogy

Scaffolding. You cannot put up the building without it, workers stand on it all day, and it is genuinely doing something load-bearing while the work is happening. Then you take it down, and it was never part of the building. If the building falls over when the scaffolding comes off, you built the wrong thing.

Your own docs already use the word — corpus_tts/ is described as “synthetic scaffolding”. The adapter is the same idea one level down.

Nothing new here — this is parts 1 to 5 assembled

taking the scaffolding down deleting the adapter before you ship
the building has to stand without it the model has to work with the adapter gone

Reading it off, piece by piece

One encoder, not two. Both lanes go through the same taped encoder. Nothing about it differs between them — it is the same 109 M numbers, and they do not move for either kind of clip. That is part 2.

The detour is only on the synthetic side. Real clips never touch the adapter, during training or after. So the fake-voice lesson from the synthetic clips has exactly one place it can land, and that place is the thing you are going to throw away.

Both lanes end at the same decoder. This is the part that has to work, and it is why part 5 mattered: because the adapter started at zero, a synthetic clip arrives at the decoder looking almost like a real one, so the word it teaches still means something when a real caller says it.

Then you delete the adapter. What ships is Typhoon's encoder, untouched, and a decoder that knows more words than it did. That is the entire deliverable.

Where this can fail — three honest ones

1. The decoder can leak. The decoder sees synthetic clips too, and nothing stops it picking up TTS-flavoured habits directly, where no adapter can catch them. Some poison reaches the half you are keeping. You would see it as real-speech accuracy getting worse as you add more synthetic — which is exactly why the amount of synthetic has to be swept rather than guessed.

2. Nothing here knows which lesson is which. No part of this machinery inspects a lesson and decides “that one is vocabulary, that one is fake voice”. The arrangement just leaves the fake-voice lesson nowhere else to go. Whether the split is actually clean is a measurement, not a guarantee.

3. It might all be unnecessary. The published claim this is built on — that transducer models like Typhoon get no benefit from synthetic audio — is one paper from 2021, and another result partly contradicts it. That is why the experiment starts with the plain untrained baseline and the naive mix, before any of this gets built.

Check yourself: what actually ships at the end?

Right — and notice how little that is. One group of 6 M numbers changed. Everything else in the design exists to make sure that is the only thing that changed.

That is what a normal adapter setup ships — mint_v4, for instance, where the adapter is the product. Here it is the opposite: the adapter is scaffolding, and shipping it would mean shipping the fake-voice lesson you spent the whole design catching.

The encoder was taped from the first step to the last — it cannot have changed. And handling synthetic audio well was never a goal; no real caller sounds like VoxCPM.
